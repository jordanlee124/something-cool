# something-cool

## Ember Vale

A hands-off colony simulator you can watch on your phone. `ember-vale.html` is the whole game: one HTML file with no build step and no dependencies apart from Google Fonts.

It is a world you watch, not one you play. When you start a new world you pick how many settlements it begins with (1–4), and they start out neutral to each other. Each settlement has its own steward AI, which assigns jobs and decides what to build and where. Each one grows through six ages, Settlement → Village → Town → City → Kingdom → Empire, and every age needs a population target plus a landmark: chapel, market, cathedral, castle, then palace.

What else happens along the way:

- **Territory:** each settlement's land grows with its age, and from the Town age on it draws a border. It can only build inside that land.
- **Politics:** every pair of settlements has its own relationship. Mood, trade and the friction of a shared border move it up or down. So do shared enemies, rivalry between the two largest powers, and political events such as royal marriages, trade pacts, border disputes, heresy and murdered envoys. Relationships can turn into alliances or wars, and wars bring raids, peace treaties and sometimes conquest.
- **Borders:** villagers can't walk through another realm's land unless the two have an open-borders treaty. Allies open their borders automatically; friendly neighbours sign treaties, and souring relations or war close them again. A villager caught on the wrong side when a border moves walks home.
- **Breakaways:** groups occasionally leave to found new settlements. These are full settlements in their own right, and the parent settlement's mood decides whether they start as friends or enemies.
- **Rulers and the chronicle:** every settlement has a named ruler with a trait (wise, just, pious, proud, weak or cruel) that affects mood and diplomacy. Rulers die and are succeeded, and they earn epithets such as "the Conqueror" or "the Brief". The Chronicle tab keeps a year-by-year history of the vale, and big moments such as wars, betrayals, conquests, collapses and plagues pop up as cards over the map.
- **Culture and religion:** every realm inherits a culture from the settlement that founded it. A realm's first chapel brings it a faith, either a neighbour's or a new one of its own, with a holy city. Faiths spread through missionaries along borders, alliances and open borders. Large faiths split in schisms. A shared faith or culture warms relations, rival faiths cool them, and zealous realms declare holy wars. The Politics tab lists the faiths, and a ring around each map label shows a realm's faith.
- **Decline and collapse:** plagues break out and spread along borders, alliances and trade. Unhappy realms revolt, disputed successions start civil wars, and aging empires can shatter into successor states. A settlement that loses its people or its landmark falls back an age.
- **An ever-expanding map:** the world is made of 16×16 chunks generated from a seed. As settlements grow toward the edge, or there is no room left to found a new one, scouts map new land and the world grows in that direction. More land allows more settlements, up to 10, while the total population stays capped at 900 to keep phones fast. The camera follows the latest big event; the "Whole world" button zooms out to show everything.
- **While you were away:** when you come back, the world catches up on the time you were gone at the speed you left it on, up to one game year, then shows what changed: which realms grew, rose, fell or were founded, and the chronicle's headlines.
- **Border replay:** the game keeps a snapshot of every realm's land each season. In the Chronicle tab, "Replay the borders" lets you scrub or play back through the map's history; the world waits while you watch.
- **Trade roads and a living economy:** every city prices food, wood and stone by its own supply and demand. A good is dear where the city has less than it wants on hand, and cheap where it piles up. Two friendly realms with markets open a named road, like the Amber Road. Caravans carry whatever sells for most at the far end compared with home and are paid in gold, or in kind when the buyer is short. Colonies trade with their capital each season along the royal road. World prices are only what caravans actually pay, and the Politics tab charts them with the cheapest and dearest city for each good. Cities collect taxes, pay their soldiers, keep up their buildings each season and burn fuel from the Industrial Era. They spend spare gold on fairs that lift the mood. Storehouses hold less than before, and a town with no rock left buys its stone. Each city's Economy panel shows its prices, stock against need, and what it bought and sold last year. War, or losing a market, closes the road.
- **Wonders and great people:** kingdoms and empires with stone to spare raise one-of-a-kind wonders (the Great Library, the Colossus, the Hanging Gardens, the Great Wall, the Sky Temple, the Grand Arena), about one every three years, each with a lasting effect. Now and then a realm produces a great general, scholar, saint, merchant or architect who serves for some years and changes how it fares. Population milestones and first winters give the early years their own entries in the chronicle.
- **Tech tree:** every realm researches on its own through seven eras, from the Tribal Era to the Modern Era, four techs per era. Techs speed up farming, work, building and caravans, strengthen armies, and soften plagues. Ideas spread faster between neighbours and trade partners, and conquerors take the loser's knowledge. The map shows it: brick and factories in the Industrial Era, apartment towers and radar posts in the Modern Era, railways and then highways on the trade roads. The Tech tab shows each realm's era, what it is studying, and the whole tree.
- **Borders hold for work too:** villagers only farm, build, quarry and fell trees on their own realm's land.
- **Built roads:** each realm's steward plans roads from its town centre to its storehouses, market, landmarks, docks and farm fields, and builds its half of every trade road. Builders lay them tile by tile for wood (plus stone from the Medieval Era). Dirt roads (+40% walking speed) become stone (+65%) and then asphalt (+90%) as the realm advances. Pathfinding is A* weighted by ground speed, so people take the roads when that is quicker. Footpaths are only wear in the grass.
- **Quarries and foresters:** realms open quarries next to rock, and a quarry's stone never runs out. A realm with no rock left digs a stone pit. Forester's huts at the edge of town fell the grown trees around them, replant each stump and plant bare ground, so wood keeps coming after the wild forest is gone.
- **Cities that grow like cities:** homes fill in from the centre outward, where land is worth most and next to other homes, and once a town has roads they are built only along them. When the frontage runs out, a side street branches off a road at the edge of town and heads outward, sometimes bending where the ground turns it. Streets keep room for two rows of homes between them. Fields form a belt just beyond the houses, and as the town grows round a field it is built over and replaced further out. Roads come near fields but not to every plot. A town may pull down one or two homes that block a road it needs, if everyone still has a roof. Only buildings at least two years old are pulled down, at most once a season per realm, and half their cost is salvaged.
- **Households and their needs:** every home is a household living on its members' wages; the best-paid families live nearest the centre, the market and the landmarks. Each household lives at a level. Getting by needs food from the granary and a warm hearth; a comfortable household also buys bread, goods, planks for repairs and tools; a well-off one adds two kinds of luxury. Households buy what their level needs at the local price, as far as the stores and their purse allow. One that keeps its needs met with savings put by moves up a level, and one that goes short slides back down (at most once a season). What households set out to buy is the demand that sets what a town wants on hand, what its trades are staffed for, and when it opens another bakery, mill, smithy, sawmill or workshop. Going short lowers the mood, comfortable lives raise it, and families who can't buy what they need move to a neighbour where they can. On the map, well-off homes get a hedged garden, comfortable ones a picket fence and window boxes, and struggling ones patches and a woodpile; up close, a household going short shows what it lacks in a thought bubble.
- **Artisans and workshops:** cities from the Town age open workshops where spare hands turn wood (and stone, from the Medieval Era) into goods. Goods are the fourth good on every market: households buy them and caravans trade them. The town's work earns the treasury the money that pays the wages.
- **Production chains:** from the Town age, grain goes to a windmill and becomes flour, and a bakery turns the flour into bread, which goes further than plain grain and lifts the mood. Sawmills turn logs into planks, which stand in for half the wood of a new building and make it go up faster. From the Town age the quarries also turn up ore, and smithies forge it into tools, which make every trade a fifth faster and wear out with use. Millers, bakers, sawyers and smiths each fetch a handcart of inputs from the storehouse, work them, and cart the better good back, so every step is a visible building and a cart trip. A chain is staffed only while there is enough to work from and its good is still wanted.
- **Regional luxuries:** wine, salt, silk and spices grow only on their own ground (vine country, salt flats, mulberry groves and spice fields), which shows on the map as patches of one kind per stretch of country. Realms plant vineyards, salt pans, silk farms and spice gardens there. Better-off households spend part of their money on luxuries, and each kind on the tables lifts the mood. A realm with no ground for a luxury pays dear for it, so caravans carry it a long way, and a realm that envies a neighbour's luxury ground grows hostile and may go to war for it.
- **Booms, busts and the crown's purse:** what a comfortable or well-off life takes rises with every era, so even big cities stay under pressure. Speculators chase a price that jumps until the bubble bursts and the well-off lose a quarter of their savings. A trade whose goods pile up and sell for less than their inputs goes bankrupt and stands boarded up for two seasons, and a hungry poor quarter riots and loots a bakery. From the City age the court costs money (more for a proud or cruel ruler and in wartime). Rich families found a merchant guild, which later builds a bank; a short treasury borrows from it at 6% a season. A crown that can't pay defaults: the rich are ruined, the guild stops lending for four years, and the ruined merchants may back a revolt. A desperate ruler with nowhere to borrow clips the coin, and every price in the city rises with it. Neighbours set tariffs on each other's goods as relations cool, trade freely as allies, and cut each other off with embargoes in war or deep enmity, which closes their trade roads.
- **Settlers, not settlements:** a new game starts with no villages at all. Two or three bands of five settlers land in different corners of the vale and walk out to look for land. Each weighs up the country near it (open ground, timber, stone, water), camps at a second place it might settle, then goes on to the better one and raises the first hall there. Tents, a fire, a banner and a handcart show where each band is, and until every band has settled the camera follows one at a time. A save made mid-trek picks up where it left off.
- **Harvests and famine:** off-season fields only just keep pace with the town, so the granary runs down from winter to summer and the year's grain comes in at the autumn harvest (sacks pile up outside the storehouses as they fill). Fields show the year: shoots in spring, a green crop in summer, gold at harvest, then stubble and stooks. Some years bring a bumper crop; now and then blight, drought, rain or locusts ruin the harvest across a whole region and spoil the stored grain too, the blighted fields turn grey-brown, grain is dear all winter, and empty granaries bring famine by spring.
- **Strikes:** when wages are cut, the coin is debased, bread is dear, rents bite or the court spends lavishly, the millers, bakers, sawyers, smiths and artisans walk out together and picket the town square with placards while red banners hang across their idle workshops. A cruel or proud ruler (or a big garrison) sends in the soldiers, who break the strike and kill some of the strikers; anyone else gives in, and wages rise for good.
- **Fairs:** in summer and autumn a market town may hold a fair. Striped tents in the guests' colours and bunting go up round the market, caravans come from every realm it knows, idle townsfolk crowd in, and speculators lose their nerve while every price is out in the open.
- **Smugglers:** where a tariff or embargo stands between two realms and a good fetches far more across the border, smugglers carry it by the back ways, off the main road. The importer's watch (stronger with more soldiers) spots some of them and runs them down, seizing the goods; the rest sell on the black market and the customs house sees nothing.
- **Land and inheritance:** from the City age, well-off families buy fields and estates from the crown or from families fallen on hard times, fly their colours over them and let them to tenants, whose rents make the rich richer. When the head of a house dies the land goes to one heir, is split among several branches, or (if the line dies out) goes back to the crown. A house that gathers enough land becomes a dynasty in the chronicle; where landlords hold most of the fields the poor resent them.
- **Road upkeep:** roads cost gold every season, more for stone and asphalt. A treasury that can't pay lets them go to potholes and weeds (and slower walking), then down to gravel or grass; a full treasury sends road gangs to mend them and resurface what wore down.
- **Colonies:** crowded kingdoms and empires send settlers 15 to 30 tiles out to found colonies, at sites with the stone, forest or water they lack (one every few years, more as they grow). A colony is a vassal: it flies its liege's colour, shares its knowledge, friends and enemies, pays tribute each season and gets a royal road to the capital. Distance, a weak or cruel liege, an unhappy or overgrown colony and the passing years wear its loyalty down, and in time it may declare independence, sometimes by war.
- **War and soldiers:** a barracks trains soldiers out of the population. Each one is a townsperson taken from work, costs gold to train and upkeep every season, and leaves their household. When a realm's soldiers are enough, it musters an army that marches along the roads toward its target, fights at the walls, then either sieges the town (starving it and killing people inside) or breaks the defenders. A conquered town's people answer to the conqueror. Armies are capped (eight at once, two per realm) so the frame rate stays steady on a phone. The AI gives every order; there are no controls. In peacetime a town keeps a standing army of about 6% of its people (14% at war), built up as the town grows (it builds another barracks when the garrison outgrows the one it has) and the last to be stood down. They still cost gold to train and upkeep every season, so a big garrison visibly drains the treasury and the workforce. Soldiers never stand about on their own: recruits who reach the barracks fall in under a banner as a formation of up to 16, and in peacetime each formation patrols its realm, walking a loop out to two points on the edge of its land and back to the barracks, where it takes in new recruits. Every formation carries its own banner in its realm's colour, with a gold trim on patrol (red at war) and an emblem that tells it from the others, and the map labels it as that realm's guard. When war comes the largest formation, with any others close by, marches out as the army, and formations at home stand with the town's defenders when an enemy reaches the walls.
- **Barbarian camps:** they appear in the unclaimed wilds, grow stronger and raid whoever is closest.
- **Seasons and mood:** every hearth burns wood in winter. Food, housing, cold, losses, landmarks, wars and allies all affect mood.
- **Controls:** speed buttons (Pause, 1×, 3×, 10×); Realm, Politics and Chronicle tabs; and tap a settlement to follow it.

The line under the map shows frames per second, the slowest recent frame, and how many milliseconds the simulation and the drawing take per frame; If the phone can't keep up, the game slows itself down rather than dropping frames, and the line says what speed it is really running at. Tap it to shorten it.

Progress saves to the browser's local storage. Saves from the earlier fixed-size map are migrated automatically.

### Code layout

The game is still one file, organised into classes: `World` (chunked tiles, terrain generation, pathfinding), `Settlement`, `Colonist`, `Steward` (the settlement AI), `Ruler`, `Camp`, `Diplomacy`, `Chronicle`, `Game` (the clock and save/load), `Renderer` (per-chunk cached drawing and the camera) and `Interface` (panels and cards).

### Run it

Open `ember-vale.html` in any browser. To play it on an iPhone, serve it from anywhere, for example GitHub Pages: Settings → Pages → deploy from the `main` branch. Then open the page in Safari and choose Share → Add to Home Screen.
